> INDIA CYBER LAW QUICK REFERENCE_

Plain-English reference for all cybercrime-related provisions under Indian law — IT Act 2000, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), and the DPDP Act 2023. For law enforcement officers, legal professionals, and security teams.

Last updated: June 2026 — Reflects BNS/BNSS/BSA in force from July 1, 2024
The Information Technology Act, 2000 remains the primary legislation for cybercrime offences in India. Despite the introduction of BNS/BNSS/BSA in 2024, the IT Act continues in force and governs unauthorized access, data theft, identity crimes, cyber terrorism, and related offences.

Investigation tip: For most cybercrime FIRs, invoke Section 66/66C/66D IT Act along with relevant BNS provisions. The IT Act gives specific cyber-focused language; BNS provides the broader criminal framework. Filing both strengthens the case.
The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) 2023 replaced the Indian Penal Code (IPC) 1860 with effect from July 1, 2024. For cybercrime investigators, the key BNS sections address fraud, cheating, personation, and criminal conspiracy in digital contexts. The IT Act remains the primary cyber law — BNS supplements it.
SectionOffence (IPC Equivalent)Plain ExplanationPunishmentType
S. 318Cheating (was IPC 420)Deceiving a person and inducing them to deliver property or valuable security. Now explicitly covers digital payment fraud — UPI, net banking, online transfers induced by deception.7 years + fineCognizable
S. 319Cheating by Personation (was IPC 416)Pretending to be someone else to cheat. Covers fake customer care, impersonation scams, fake government officials calling for "digital arrest." Stronger with S.66D IT Act.3 years + fineCognizable
S. 316Criminal Breach of Trust (was IPC 405/406)Misappropriation of property entrusted — relevant for insider data theft, employee misuse of company systems/data, crypto custodian fraud.3–7 years + fineCognizable
S. 336Forgery (was IPC 463)Making false documents or electronic records with intent to cause damage or fraud. Covers fabricated digital evidence, forged email headers, fake KYC documents.2 years + fineBailable
S. 338Forgery for Fraud (was IPC 468)Forgery specifically for the purpose of cheating — higher punishment than basic forgery. Used when forged digital documents are used in fraud schemes.7 years + fineCognizable
S. 340Using Forged Documents (was IPC 471)Using a forged document as genuine, knowing it to be forged. Covers use of fabricated bank statements, fake KYC, forged digital signatures.Same as forgery itselfCognizable
S. 111Organised CrimeNEW provision — organized crime syndicates, criminal enterprises. Covers coordinated cyber fraud networks, ransomware groups, SIM swap rings operating as organized networks.Life / Death + fineCognizable
S. 61Criminal Conspiracy (was IPC 120B)Agreement between two or more persons to commit any offence. Invoked against all members of a cyber fraud network — even those not directly committing the final act.Same as offence conspiredCognizable
S. 308Extortion (was IPC 383/384)Putting someone in fear to dishonestly induce delivery of property. Covers ransomware demands, sextortion, threatening to leak data unless payment made.3–10 years + fineCognizable
S. 351Criminal Intimidation (was IPC 503/506)Threatening someone with injury to person, reputation, or property. Covers online threats, cyber harassment, threatening messages on WhatsApp/social media.2–7 years + fineBailable
FIR drafting note: After July 1, 2024, cite BNS sections, not IPC. Courts have noted procedural irregularities when old IPC sections are cited in new FIRs. Example: Instead of "Section 420 IPC" write "Section 318 BNS 2023."
The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) 2023 replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC) 1973 from July 1, 2024. It governs investigation powers, court procedures, evidence production, and arrest/bail provisions. Key changes relevant to cyber investigations are highlighted.
SectionPower/Provision (CrPC Equivalent)Explanation for Investigators
S. 94Production of Documents (was CrPC 91)Court or officer can direct any person to produce documents or electronic records. Primary tool for serving legal process on banks, telecom operators, ISPs, social media platforms. Must specify the documents needed and legal basis.
S. 105Search and Seizure (was CrPC 100)NEW: Mandatory audio-video recording of all search and seizure operations. Failure to record may affect admissibility. This creates both an evidentiary benefit and compliance obligation for cyber cell raids.
S. 176FIR Registration (was CrPC 154)NEW: Zero FIR can be filed at any police station regardless of jurisdiction. Within 15 days, FIR must be transferred to the appropriate jurisdiction. Critical for cybercrime cases where offence may cross multiple jurisdictions.
S. 173Investigation (was CrPC 156)Police power to investigate cognizable offences. For cyber cases: investigation includes digital evidence collection, device seizure, and online platform legal process. Timeline for charge sheet filing: 60 days (custody) or 90 days (bail).
S. 532Trial via Electronic ModeNEW: Witnesses can testify via electronic means (video conferencing). Reduces delay in cyber cases where witnesses or experts are in different cities. Evidence can be submitted electronically to courts.
S. 187Remand (was CrPC 167)Custody of accused during investigation — maximum 15 days police custody, maximum 60/90 days judicial custody before charge sheet. For cyber cases: device forensics must be completed within this window.
S. 106Provisional AttachmentNEW/enhanced: Provisional attachment of property linked to crime — faster than previous process, does not require court order in all cases. Critical for freezing mule accounts and crypto wallets before funds are moved.
S. 185Arrest Without WarrantPolice can arrest without warrant for cognizable offences. For cybercrime: when accused is identified, immediate arrest prevents evidence destruction. Section 66/66C/66D IT Act are all cognizable — arrest without warrant is valid.
Section 94 BNSS vs Section 91 CrPC: These are functionally equivalent — legal notices issued before July 1, 2024 cite S.91 CrPC, those after cite S.94 BNSS. Banks, telecom operators, and platforms should respond to both. If a platform refuses a post-July 2024 notice citing S.91, serve a fresh notice citing S.94 BNSS.
The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) 2023 replaced the Indian Evidence Act 1872 from July 1, 2024. The most critical change for digital forensics professionals and cybercrime investigators is the updated Section 63 certificate requirement for electronic evidence.
SectionProvision (IEA Equivalent)What Changed and Why It Matters
S. 63Electronic Evidence Certificate (was IEA 65B)The certificate authenticating electronic evidence must now include the HASH VALUE of the electronic record. This is the most critical change — Section 65B certificates without hash values are now non-compliant. The certificate must be signed by the responsible official of the device or certifying expert.
S. 57Documents in Evidence (was IEA 61)Electronic records are explicitly recognized as documents admissible in evidence. Covers: emails, WhatsApp messages, social media posts, digital photographs, CCTV footage, transaction records, log files.
S. 22Electronic AgreementsElectronic contracts and agreements are valid. Relevant for cybercrimes involving digital consent, online agreements, electronic signatures.
S. 59Presumption as to Electronic RecordsCourt may presume that an electronic record purporting to be an agreement was concluded in the manner appearing on the record. Useful for email and WhatsApp-based fraud evidence.
S. 60Electronic Signature PresumptionCourt shall presume that a secure electronic signature is the subscriber's signature. Strengthens admissibility of electronically signed documents in fraud cases.
Section 63 BSA 2023 Certificate — Mandatory Elements:

1. The computer/device was in proper working order during the relevant period
2. The electronic record was produced in the ordinary course of activities
3. The information was regularly fed into the computer during that period
4. NEW: The hash value of the electronic record (SHA-256 recommended)
5. Signed by the responsible official or forensic examiner

Practical action: Update all forensic report templates to include SHA-256 hash values. Courts are beginning to reject Section 63/65B certificates without hash values.
The Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (DPDP Act) is India's first comprehensive data protection legislation. It governs how organizations collect, process, and protect personal data. Rules are pending finalization — but the Act's obligations are in force. The Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) will enforce it.
ProvisionObligationPractical Impact
S. 4Grounds for ProcessingPersonal data can only be processed with consent or for legitimate uses (legal obligation, state function, public interest, employment). Consent must be free, specific, informed, and unconditional.
S. 8Data Fiduciary ObligationsOrganizations processing personal data must: implement appropriate security safeguards, erase data when purpose is fulfilled, notify the Data Protection Board and affected data principals of personal data breaches.
S. 9Children's DataProcessing data of children under 18 requires verifiable parental consent. No targeted advertising to children. No tracking children's online behavior. Significant implications for social media and ed-tech platforms.
S. 17Data Principal RightsIndividuals have rights to: access their data, correct inaccurate data, erasure of data (right to be forgotten), and grievance redressal. Organizations must have a mechanism to respond to these requests.
S. 25PenaltiesFailure to prevent data breach: up to Rs 250 crore. Failure to notify breach: up to Rs 200 crore. Violation of children's data provisions: up to Rs 200 crore. Non-compliance with Data Protection Board directions: up to Rs 150 crore.
S. 17(2)Exemptions for Law EnforcementThe DPDP Act exempts processing necessary for prevention, detection, investigation, or prosecution of offences. Law enforcement agencies are not required to comply with data access, correction, or erasure requests during active investigations.
For corporate security teams: The DPDP Act breach notification requirement works alongside CERT-In's 6-hour mandatory reporting direction. Both apply simultaneously. When a data breach occurs: CERT-In within 6 hours + Data Protection Board notification (timeline per Rules, expected 72 hours). Start building your breach response playbook now — do not wait for Rules to be finalized.

Zero FIR guidance: Under BNSS Section 176, any police station must register a Zero FIR for cognizable cybercrime offences regardless of jurisdiction. The FIR must be transferred to the appropriate jurisdiction within 15 days. Victim should insist on Zero FIR registration if local police claim lack of jurisdiction.

Applying India’s New Criminal Codes in Practice

As of 2024 India’s criminal law operates under three new codes — the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, all of 2023 — which replace the Indian Penal Code, the Code of Criminal Procedure, and the Indian Evidence Act respectively. For cyber investigations these work alongside, and do not displace, the Information Technology Act, 2000, which remains the primary statute for computer-specific offences.

In day-to-day practice this means mapping each element of a case to the correct provision: substantive offences such as cheating and impersonation under the BNS, computer-specific offences under the IT Act, production of records from intermediaries under the BNSS, and the certification of electronic evidence under the BSA. A common error worth avoiding is any reliance on Section 66A of the IT Act, which was struck down by the Supreme Court in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) and is no longer valid law. This reference is maintained to reflect the provisions as they currently stand.
