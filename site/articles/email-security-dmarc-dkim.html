Email Authentication: Implementing SPF, DKIM and DMARC to Stop Domain Spoofing

File No.IGRS-057/2026
SubjectTechnical implementation guide for email authentication standards for Indian organizations
CategoryThreat Intelligence
AuthorManuj Kumar Pandey
Published2026-05-27
Reading time6 minutes

Spoofing your organization's email domain is trivially easy without authentication controls. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC together stop it. Implementation guide for Indian IT teams.

The domain spoofing problem

Without email authentication controls, anyone can send email claiming to be from your-organization.com. This requires no system access, no credentials, and no technical skill beyond configuring a mail server. It enables CEO fraud impersonating your executive to authorize payments, spear phishing from your IT team or HR department, and reputational damage when your domain is used to send spam or phishing to others.

SPF (Sender Policy Framework)

SPF is a DNS TXT record specifying which servers are authorized to send email on behalf of your domain. When a receiving server gets a message claiming to be from your domain, it checks whether the sending server's IP is on the authorized list. Implementation: identify all servers and services that send email for your domain (mail server, marketing platforms, CRM, ticketing systems), find their sending IPs, and publish them in a TXT record: v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ip4:203.0.113.10 -all. The -all at the end specifies that messages from unlisted sources should be rejected.

DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail)

DKIM adds a cryptographic signature to outgoing emails that receiving servers verify. It proves the message originated from a server with the private key and was not modified in transit. Implementation requires generating a key pair, configuring your mail server to sign messages, and publishing the public key in DNS. Most cloud email providers (Google Workspace, Microsoft 365) provide guided DKIM setup in their admin consoles.

DMARC

DMARC ties SPF and DKIM together and specifies what happens to messages that fail authentication. Start with p=none and a reporting address (rua=mailto:dmarc@yourdomain.com). Review aggregate reports to understand your legitimate email flows. Then progressively move to p=quarantine then p=reject.

The goal is DMARC at p=reject — full enforcement blocking all spoofed messages. Rushing to reject without first monitoring legitimate sending sources with p=none will block legitimate email from services your organization uses and is not aware of. Monitor first, then enforce.
