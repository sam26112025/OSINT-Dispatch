India's Cybercrime Law in 2026: BNS, BNSS, BSA and What Changed

File No.IGRS-025/2026
SubjectUpdated overview of India's cybercrime legal framework under the new criminal codes
CategoryLaw & Evidence
AuthorManuj Kumar Pandey
Published2026-05-05
Reading time7 minutes

India replaced its colonial-era criminal codes in 2023–24. Here is what changed for cybercrime prosecution, digital evidence, and investigation powers — and what stayed the same.

The legislative transition

India's three foundational criminal laws — the Indian Penal Code (1860), the Code of Criminal Procedure (1973), and the Indian Evidence Act (1872) — were replaced in 2023–24 by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) respectively. These came into force on July 1, 2024. For cybercrime investigators, practitioners, and legal professionals, understanding what changed — and what did not — is essential to current practice.

Cybercrime provisions: continuity and change

The BNS does not replace the Information Technology Act, 2000, which remains the primary legislation for most cybercrime offences including unauthorised access (Section 66), identity theft (66C), cheating by personation (66D), publishing obscene material (67), and cyber terrorism (66F). What the BNS does is modernise the general offences that intersect with cybercrime — criminal conspiracy, cheating, fraud, criminal intimidation, and extortion — with updated language that better accommodates digital contexts. Section 318 BNS (cheating) now explicitly covers digital payment fraud. Section 319 covers personation in digital identity contexts.

Investigation powers under BNSS

The BNSS introduces or clarifies several powers relevant to cyber investigations. Section 94 (previously CrPC 91) governs production of documents and electronic records — the primary tool for serving legal process on platforms, banks, and telecom providers. Section 105 creates a provisional attachment mechanism that is faster than the previous process, useful for freezing accounts before funds move. A new audio-video recording requirement for search and seizure under Section 105 creates both an evidentiary benefit (recorded procedures are harder to challenge) and a compliance burden (every search requires recording equipment and trained operators).

Digital evidence under BSA

The BSA is where the most significant changes for digital investigations occur. Section 63 (successor to IEA Section 65B) maintains the certificate requirement for secondary electronic evidence but adds a new element: the certificate must now include a hash value of the electronic record, signed by the certifying expert. This brings Indian evidence law closer to international forensic standards and closes a gap that defence counsel had previously exploited to challenge electronic evidence where no hash had been computed.

Section 63 BSA certificates must now include hash values. Forensic teams producing electronic evidence for court should update their standard operating procedures immediately if this has not already been done.

What did not change

The IT Act's Section 43A (compensation for failure to protect sensitive personal data), Section 69 (government powers to intercept and monitor), Section 69B (monitoring of traffic data), and Section 70 (protection of critical information infrastructure) are unchanged. The Aadhaar Act's restrictions on identity information access are unchanged. CERT-In's mandatory incident reporting directions (6-hour reporting window) are unchanged and continue to carry the force of law under the IT Act framework.

Practical implications

For investigation teams: update your Section 63/65B certificate templates to include hash values. Review legal notice templates to reference BNSS sections rather than CrPC. Ensure search and seizure procedures include audio-video recording capability. For legal teams: verify that briefings and submissions reference the correct new section numbers — courts are beginning to flag references to the old codes as procedural irregularities.
