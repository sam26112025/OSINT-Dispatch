How to Spot a Phishing Email: A Field Guide

File No.OD-003/2026
SubjectBehavioural and technical indicators of phishing, with verification workflow
CategoryPrivacy & Safety
AuthorManuj Kumar Pandey
Published2026-04-22
Reading time6 minutes

Phishing remains the most common entry point for fraud and account takeover. These are the indicators that actually work — beyond 'check for spelling mistakes'.

Why the old advice fails

For years users were told to look for bad grammar and obvious typos. Modern phishing kits — and now AI-written lures — produce flawless copy in any language. The reliable indicators today are structural and behavioural, not cosmetic. Here is what to actually check.

1. Pressure is the signature

Nearly every phishing message manufactures urgency or fear: your account will be suspended in 24 hours; a payment failed; a parcel is held at customs; legal action has been initiated; your KYC has expired. Legitimate organisations rarely combine a threat with a deadline and a link. When you feel the pull to act right now, that feeling itself is the indicator. Slow down — urgency is the attacker's primary tool because it bypasses careful reading.

2. Inspect the real sender, not the display name

The display name ("State Bank Customer Care") is free text — anyone can set it. Check the actual address behind it. Watch for:

  • Lookalike domains: sbicare-support.com instead of sbi.co.in; characters swapped (rn for m, 1 for l).
  • Free webmail: a bank or government body does not contact you from a Gmail or Outlook address.
  • Subdomain tricks: sbi.co.in.verify-account.example.com is owned by example.com, not SBI. The domain that matters is the one immediately before the final extension.

3. Hover before you click — or do not click at all

On desktop, hovering over a link shows the true destination in the status bar; on mobile, a long-press reveals it. The displayed text and the actual URL frequently differ. Better still: never log in through an emailed link. If a message claims a problem with your bank, telecom or government account, open the official app or type the known website address yourself. This single habit defeats almost all credential phishing.

4. Attachment red flags

  • Unexpected invoices, receipts or "scanned documents" — especially compressed files (.zip, .rar) or files asking you to "enable content"/"enable macros" after opening.
  • Double extensions like statement.pdf.exe.
  • HTML attachments that open a login page — a common technique for bypassing link scanners.

If an attachment is unexpected, verify with the sender through a separate channel (a phone call to a number you already have — not a number in the email) before opening.

5. The India-specific patterns

Indian users currently see heavy volumes of these themes: electricity-bill disconnection SMS, fake KYC-update messages for banks and wallets, courier/customs duty demands, income-tax refund lures, and "digital arrest" calls claiming you are implicated in a crime. All share the same skeleton: authority + fear + deadline + payment or credentials. No genuine Indian agency demands payment over a chat app, asks you to stay on a video call, or threatens immediate arrest by phone.

A 30-second verification workflow

  1. Pause. Did I expect this message?
  2. Sender. Expand and read the real address and domain.
  3. Link. Long-press/hover — does the domain match the organisation exactly?
  4. Channel-switch. If it concerns money or credentials, verify via the official app or a known phone number.
  5. Report. Use your email provider's report-phishing button; in India, forward fraud SMS/calls to the Chakshu portal (sancharsaathi.gov.in) and report financial fraud at 1930.
If you already clicked: change the password of the affected account immediately from a clean device, enable two-factor authentication, and if money or banking details are involved, call 1930 (Indian national cyber fraud helpline) without delay — early reporting dramatically improves fund-recovery chances.
