Quishing: Why QR Code Phishing Is Exploding in India — And How to Spot It

India runs on the QR code. We scan to pay the chaiwala, the parking attendant, the kirana store and the toll booth — often without a second thought. That reflex is exactly what a fast-growing class of scam is built to exploit. Quishing — QR-code phishing — swaps the suspicious link for an innocent-looking square of dots, and it is now prominent enough that Google's mid-2026 scams advisory called out QR-code phishing and calendar-based lures as rising techniques worldwide. For a country that scans billions of UPI QR codes a month, the attack surface is enormous. Here is how the scam works, why it lands so easily here, and how to defend against it.

What quishing actually is

A QR code is just a machine-readable way to store a URL or a payment instruction. Quishing hides a malicious destination inside that code. Because there is no visible link to inspect, the usual advice — “hover before you click” — does not apply. When the victim scans, one of a few things happens: a convincing but fake login page loads to harvest credentials, a fraudulent payment page opens, or a UPI collect request is triggered that, if approved, pulls money out of the victim's account. The code launders the attacker's intent behind a format we have been trained to trust.

Why India is a prime target

Three things stack up. First, ubiquity: scanning a QR to pay is muscle memory, so a tampered or fake code rarely raises suspicion. Second, physical placement: fraudsters paste their own QR stickers over genuine ones on parking meters, fuel pumps, restaurant tables and electricity-bill notices — the victim believes they are paying a legitimate merchant. Third, filter evasion: in email and chat, a QR image sails past link-scanners that would have flagged a raw URL, which is why corporate inboxes are now seeing “scan to view your payslip / re-validate your account” lures. Layered on top is a classic UPI misunderstanding many users still hold — that you must scan or enter a PIN to receive money. You never do.

How the scam typically runs

  • The bait. A QR arrives with urgency or reward attached — a refund, a KYC re-verification, a parking fine, a prize, a courier redelivery.
  • The scan. The code opens a look-alike page (bank, wallet, government portal) or a UPI request. Domains are near-identical to the real thing.
  • The capture. The victim enters credentials or OTPs, or approves a collect request — handing over either access or money.
  • The cash-out. Funds are quickly moved through a chain of mule accounts, making recovery a race against time.

Red flags to train yourself on

  • A QR that asks you to scan to receive a refund, prize or payment — receiving money never requires scanning or a PIN.
  • A sticker QR that looks pasted over the original, or a code sent by an unknown number with pressure to act fast.
  • A scanned link whose domain doesn't match the organisation it claims to be (look closely for misspellings and extra words).
  • Any request to enter your UPI PIN after scanning — the PIN authorises money leaving your account, never money coming in.

How to stay safe

Use a scanner (most phone cameras now do this) that previews the decoded URL before opening it, and read that URL like you would any link. Treat physical QR codes with the same suspicion as a stranger's USB stick — if a sticker looks added on, pay through the merchant's app or by name instead. Never approve a UPI collect request you did not initiate. And keep your phone's built-in scam warnings switched on; modern Android and iOS builds increasingly flag suspicious destinations.

For investigators: analysing a malicious QR safely

Treat the QR as untrusted input. Decode it to plain text without letting the device navigate — an offline decoder or a sandboxed environment surfaces the embedded URL or UPI string for examination. From there the workflow is familiar OSINT: profile the destination domain and hosting with the IGRS IP Lookup, examine any associated phishing email with the Email Header Analyzer, and map the wider campaign infrastructure. For UPI strings, the VPA and payee name are the pivots for a money-trail request. Preserve the original code, the decoded payload and timestamps as evidence.

Legal framing & reporting (India)

A quishing operation typically engages several provisions: Section 318 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (cheating) where money is lost, Section 319 BNS (cheating by personation) for the impersonation of a bank or brand, and Section 66D of the IT Act, 2000 (cheating by personation using a computer resource). Tracing the destination infrastructure or the UPI trail supports a production request under Section 94 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, and electronic evidence should carry a certificate under Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023. Victims should call 1930 and file at cybercrime.gov.in immediately — speed is what makes a freeze possible.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is quishing?
Quishing is phishing that uses a QR code instead of a clickable link. The code hides a malicious URL; scanning it opens a fake login or payment page, or triggers a UPI collect request designed to take money or credentials.
Why is QR code phishing a big problem in India?
India scans QR codes constantly for UPI payments, so a fake or tampered QR feels normal. Attackers exploit that habit by placing malicious codes on posters, parking meters, fake refund pages and emails.
How can I check if a QR code is safe before acting?
Preview the decoded URL before opening it, confirm the domain matches the real organisation, and never approve a UPI request you did not initiate — you never need to scan or enter a PIN to receive money, only to send it.
What should I do if I fell for a QR scam?
Call 1930 immediately and file a complaint at cybercrime.gov.in. Fast reporting improves the chance of freezing the transaction. Preserve the QR, message and screenshots as evidence.
