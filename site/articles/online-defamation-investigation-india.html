Online Defamation and Cyber Harassment Investigation — India 2026

Online Defamation and Harassment in India

Online defamation, cyber harassment, and coordinated trolling have become serious crises in India. From malicious reviews destroying businesses to coordinated campaigns targeting individuals to non-consensual content used for extortion, the harm is real and growing. Understanding the legal framework and investigation methodology is essential for both victims seeking justice and investigators building cases. This guide covers the complete approach under Indian law.

The Legal Framework

OffenceSectionAct
DefamationSection 356BNS 2023
Criminal intimidation onlineSection 351BNS 2023
Obscene content onlineSection 67IT Act 2000
Sexually explicit contentSection 67AIT Act 2000
StalkingSection 78BNS 2023
VoyeurismSection 77BNS 2023

A vital point: Section 66A of the IT Act was struck down by the Supreme Court in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) and must never be used in any FIR. Cases are sometimes wrongly registered under it, which can lead to them being quashed.

Evidence Preservation — The Critical First Step

Defamatory content can be deleted instantly, so preservation is the absolute priority:

  • Screenshot all content immediately, with visible URLs and timestamps
  • Use Hunchly or SingleFile for complete webpage capture
  • Archive URLs via web.archive.org/save
  • Record exact URLs, usernames, and post times
  • Compute SHA-256 hashes of all captures
  • Request a Section 63 BSA certificate from the platform where possible

Platform Reporting and Content Removal

Under the IT Rules 2021, intermediaries must act on flagged unlawful content within defined timelines. Significant Social Media Intermediaries are required to have an India-based Grievance Officer. The reporting sequence is: report the content for removal through the platform, escalate to the Grievance Officer if needed, and if no action follows, complain to the Ministry of Electronics and IT. Documenting each step strengthens any subsequent legal action.

Identifying Anonymous Abusers

Many online abusers hide behind anonymity, but several techniques unmask them:

  • Username cross-referencing — the same handle on other platforms
  • Writing-style analysis — vocabulary, grammar, and phrasing patterns
  • Timing patterns — activity hours suggesting timezone and routine
  • Network analysis — who the account interacts with
  • Platform legal process — registration IP, creation date, linked phone

The combination of behavioural OSINT and legal data frequently exposes anonymous abusers.

The Legal Process for Account Data

To obtain authoritative identity data, investigators serve legal process on the platform. Meta, Twitter/X, Google, and LinkedIn all have law enforcement portals that accept Indian legal requests under Section 94 BNSS. The data provided — registration IP, account creation details, linked phone number — connects an anonymous account to a real identity, which can then be traced through ISP records to a subscriber.

Handling Non-Consensual Imagery

Cases involving non-consensual intimate imagery require special sensitivity and speed. Victims should use the confidential reporting channels for crimes against women and children on cybercrime.gov.in, which allow anonymous reporting. Content removal under IT Rules 2021 is urgent to limit spread. The applicable sections include 67/67A IT Act and relevant BNS provisions. Preserving evidence while protecting the victim's dignity is the careful balance investigators must strike.

Coordinated Trolling and Campaigns

Coordinated harassment campaigns — multiple accounts targeting an individual — require network analysis to distinguish organic criticism from orchestrated abuse. Indicators include synchronised timing, similar phrasing across accounts, shared creation dates, and interconnected networks. Identifying the coordination, and potentially the organiser, transforms scattered abuse into a prosecutable conspiracy.

Building the Case for Court

A defamation or harassment case succeeds when evidence is preserved and certified properly, the correct current legal sections are applied (never the struck-down 66A), the perpetrator is identified through OSINT and legal process, and the chain of custody is documented. The combination of solid evidence handling and accurate legal framing is what allows courts to act against online abuse.

Support for Victims

Beyond investigation, victims of online defamation and harassment should know their options: immediate content reporting and removal, complaint at cybercrime.gov.in, FIR with correct sections, and where appropriate, civil defamation action for damages. The psychological toll of online abuse is significant, and a swift, competent response — both removing the content and pursuing the perpetrator — is the most effective remedy the system can provide.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the law for online defamation in India?
Section 353 BNS 2023 covers defamation (replaced IPC 499/500). For online specific harassment, Section 67 IT Act covers obscene content, Section 78 BNS covers stalking, and Section 351 BNS covers criminal intimidation. Section 66A is struck down and cannot be used.
Can I file FIR for trolling and harassment on social media?
Yes. Online harassment, threats, and defamation are covered under BNS and IT Act sections. File FIR at cyber cell with all evidence preserved (screenshots with timestamps, URLs). Cybercrime.gov.in also accepts online complaints.
Can anonymous social media accounts be traced in India?
Yes, with proper legal process. Law enforcement can request platform account registration data (IP, phone number, creation date) through Meta, Twitter/X, Google LEA portals. IP addresses can be traced to subscriber through ISP records.
