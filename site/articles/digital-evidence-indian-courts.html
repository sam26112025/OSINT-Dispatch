How Digital Evidence Works in Indian Courts: A Plain-Language Guide

File No.OD-008/2026
SubjectAdmissibility framework for electronic records under Indian law (BSA 2023)
CategoryLaw & Evidence
AuthorManuj Kumar Pandey
Published2026-05-26
Reading time7 minutes

Screenshots, chats, CCTV and call records decide cases every day — but only if they are legally admissible. How Indian law treats electronic evidence, explained without jargon.

Why this matters to ordinary people

Digital evidence is no longer a niche topic for lawyers. WhatsApp chats appear in matrimonial disputes, CCTV footage in accident claims, call detail records in criminal trials, and email trails in employment cases. Whether that evidence counts depends on rules most people have never heard of — and cases are routinely weakened because someone preserved evidence the wrong way.

The legal framework, briefly

India's evidence law was overhauled in 2023–24. The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA) replaced the colonial-era Indian Evidence Act, 1872. For electronic records, the centre of gravity is Section 63 of the BSA, which carries forward (with refinements) the regime previously found in the famous Section 65B of the old Act. The core idea is unchanged:

An electronic record produced in court as a printout or on media (a copy, that is — not the original device) is admissible only if accompanied by a certificate identifying the record, describing the device that produced it, and confirming the device was operating properly — signed by a person in responsible charge of that device. The BSA's certificate format also incorporates an expert's part with a hash value of the record.

The Supreme Court's decision in Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (2020) settled years of confusion by holding the certificate mandatory for secondary electronic evidence. Courts have continued to apply that strictness under the new code.

What this means in practice

  • A bare screenshot is fragile evidence. It can be admitted in some circumstances, but without a proper certificate and supporting material, its evidentiary weight is low and it is easily challenged as fabricated.
  • The original device is gold. Producing the actual phone or computer (primary evidence) avoids many certificate complications. Never wipe, "clean up", factory-reset or repair a device that may matter to a dispute.
  • Hash values matter. A hash is a digital fingerprint of a file; if even one bit changes, the hash changes. Forensic practice — and now the statutory certificate format — uses hashes to prove a copy is identical to the original.
  • Chain of custody is the silent killer. Evidence must be accounted for from seizure to courtroom. Gaps — who held the device, when, what was done to it — are exactly where opposing counsel attacks.

If you are preserving evidence yourself

Before professionals get involved, ordinary parties can avoid the classic mistakes:

  1. Do not edit anything. Do not crop, annotate, rename or forward-as-new. Keep originals exactly as received.
  2. Preserve in place. Keep the chats/emails on the original device and account; export full threads (with dates visible), not isolated bubbles.
  3. Capture context. Full-screen captures showing sender, number/handle, date and time outweigh cropped fragments.
  4. Move fast on third-party data. CCTV systems typically overwrite within days or weeks; telecom and platform records have retention limits. A prompt written preservation request — or a police complaint that triggers one — can be decisive.
  5. Get professional imaging for high-stakes matters. A forensic examiner creates a verified, hash-matched image of the device so analysis never touches the original.

The expert's role

Under the BSA, the opinion of an Examiner of Electronic Evidence (notified under Section 79A of the IT Act) is relevant expert testimony on digital records. In practice, forensic reports stand or fall on methodology: what tool was used, whether write-blocking was employed, whether hashes were recorded, and whether the report's conclusions stay within what the data actually shows. Overstated conclusions are dismantled in cross-examination.

This article is general legal information, not legal advice. For any live dispute, consult a qualified advocate — procedural details and recent case law matter enormously in this area.
