What Is Threat Intelligence? IOCs, TTPs and Feeds, Explained Simply

File No.OD-010/2026
SubjectCyber threat intelligence concepts: levels, indicators, frameworks, lifecycle
CategoryThreat Intelligence
AuthorManuj Kumar Pandey
Published2026-06-05
Reading time6 minutes

'Threat intelligence' is one of the most used and least understood terms in cybersecurity. What it actually means, the difference between an IOC and a TTP, and why context is everything.

Data, information, intelligence

The security industry sells "threat intelligence" on everything, so the term has blurred. The clean definition: threat intelligence is analysed knowledge about adversaries — their capabilities, infrastructure, methods and intent — produced to support a specific defensive decision. The hierarchy matters: a raw malicious IP address is data; that IP tied to a phishing campaign is information; an assessment that the campaign targets Indian banking customers via fake KYC pages, with indicators your team should block and patterns your staff should watch for — that is intelligence. The difference is context plus relevance plus actionability.

The three levels

  • Strategic — for leadership: threat landscape trends, sector targeting, risk to the business. Informs budgets and policy. ("Ransomware groups are increasingly targeting Indian manufacturing.")
  • Operational — for security managers: details of specific campaigns and adversary groups, their tooling and infrastructure. Informs defensive priorities and hunts.
  • Tactical/technical — for the SOC and its tools: concrete indicators to detect and block, right now.

IOCs vs TTPs — the distinction that matters most

Indicators of Compromise (IOCs) are forensic artefacts of malicious activity: file hashes, malicious domains and IPs, registry keys, sender addresses. They are precise but perishable — an attacker can re-hash malware or rotate a domain in minutes.

TTPs — Tactics, Techniques and Procedures — describe adversary behaviour: how they gain initial access, escalate privileges, move laterally, exfiltrate. Behaviour is expensive to change. This asymmetry is captured in David Bianco's "Pyramid of Pain": blocking a hash barely inconveniences an attacker; detecting their techniques forces them to retool. Mature defence therefore climbs the pyramid — from chasing indicators toward detecting behaviours.

The industry's shared vocabulary for TTPs is the MITRE ATT&CK framework, a public, continuously updated matrix of adversary techniques observed in the real world. When a report says an actor used "T1566 — Phishing", every defender on earth knows precisely what is meant.

Where intelligence comes from

  • Open sources (OSINT): vendor research blogs, CERT advisories (in India, CERT-In alerts), abuse databases, public sandboxes.
  • Internal telemetry: your own logs, incidents and phishing reports — the most relevant source any organisation has, and the most neglected.
  • Commercial feeds and platforms: curated indicators and finished reporting, valuable when matched to your sector.
  • Sharing communities: sector ISACs and trust groups where peers exchange sightings.
  • Dark-web monitoring: lawful observation of criminal markets and forums for stolen data and access being traded.

Standards like STIX (a structured format for describing threat information) and TAXII (a protocol for exchanging it), plus open platforms like MISP, let all of this flow between organisations and tools automatically.

The lifecycle — and the step everyone skips

Good intelligence programmes run a cycle: define requirements (what decisions need supporting?), collect, process, analyse, disseminate, and gather feedback. The step organisations skip is the first one. Without explicit requirements — "we need early warning of credential leaks affecting our domains" — teams drown in generic feeds that generate alerts nobody acts on. Intelligence that supports no decision is just expensive noise.

For students: the fastest way to build genuine TI skill costs nothing — read vendor research reports actively, map each described attack to MITRE ATT&CK techniques yourself, and write one-page summaries answering "who, targeting whom, how, and what should a defender do". That exercise is the actual job.
