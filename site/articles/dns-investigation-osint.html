DNS Investigation: Reading the Internet's Phone Book for OSINT Intelligence

File No.IGRS-049/2026
SubjectDNS analysis techniques for domain investigation, infrastructure mapping, and threat actor attribution
CategoryOSINT Fundamentals
AuthorManuj Kumar Pandey
Published2026-05-03
Reading time5 minutes

DNS records contain significant intelligence about how an organization or threat actor operates online. How to read, analyse, and use them in investigations.

Why DNS matters for investigators

DNS is the internet's address book — translating domain names into IP addresses and routing email. Every domain's DNS configuration is public, and the records it contains reveal substantial intelligence about the organization behind it: hosting infrastructure, email provider, security configuration, and sometimes subdomains not publicly advertised. For threat actor investigation, DNS records and their historical versions are primary attribution and infrastructure-mapping tools.

DNS record types and what they reveal

A record: Maps a domain to an IPv4 address. The IP reveals the hosting provider and may link to other domains on the same server. MX record: Identifies the mail server — reveals email provider (Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, in-house). TXT record: Contains SPF records, DMARC policy, DKIM public keys, and domain verification records for third-party services — each verification record reveals a service the organization uses. NS record: Identifies name servers — sometimes correlates with other domains managed by the same actor. CNAME: Aliases revealing CDN providers or SaaS services in use.

Investigation tools

MXToolbox provides comprehensive DNS lookups across all record types. SecurityTrails and PassiveTotal provide historical DNS data — what IP addresses a domain pointed to in the past. VirusTotal's domain report combines DNS data with threat intelligence. DNSdumpster for subdomain enumeration. Command-line: dig on Linux/Mac, nslookup on Windows.

Historical DNS for attribution

Threat actors rotate domains and IPs to evade blocklists. Historical DNS databases record past IP resolutions — and which other domains shared those IPs. Pivoting from a known malicious domain to its historical IPs, then to other domains that pointed to those same IPs, reveals the actor's broader infrastructure across multiple campaigns.

Subdomain enumeration via DNS brute-forcing against a target is active reconnaissance — only appropriate in authorized security assessments. Certificate transparency logs (crt.sh) and SecurityTrails are appropriate passive alternatives for open-source investigation.
