India's DPDP Act 2026 — What Cybercrime Investigators Need to Know

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (DPDP Act), notified in August 2023 and with rules expected in 2026, represents India's first comprehensive data protection legislation. For cybercrime investigators, it creates both new investigative tools and new compliance obligations. Understanding what the DPDP Act means for investigations — what it permits, what it restricts, and what exemptions apply — is now essential knowledge.

What is the DPDP Act?

The DPDP Act governs the processing of digital personal data of Indian residents. It establishes rights for data principals (individuals) and obligations for data fiduciaries (entities processing data). Violations attract penalties up to ₹250 crore per instance.

Key Definitions for Investigators

  • Personal data: Any data about an identifiable individual — name, phone number, Aadhaar, email, IP address, location data
  • Data fiduciary: Entity that determines the purpose and means of processing — banks, telecom companies, social media platforms
  • Consent manager: Registered entity through which individuals can manage consent — new ecosystem being created
  • Data Protection Board: Adjudicatory body under the Act — receives complaints, imposes penalties

Exemptions for Law Enforcement

Section 17 of the DPDP Act provides critical exemptions for state instrumentalities:

  • Central and state governments can exempt any instrumentality of the state from provisions of the Act in the interest of sovereignty, security, or public order
  • Processing for prevention, detection, investigation, and prosecution of offences is exempt from most consent requirements
  • Courts and tribunals are exempt
  • Research, archiving, and statistical purposes are exempt with safeguards

Impact on Investigation Requests

Requests to Private Companies

When investigators issue Section 91 BNSS notices to companies for user data, the DPDP Act does not override lawful court orders and government orders. However, companies now have clearer obligations to log all such disclosures and notify affected users where permissible. Expect companies — especially international platforms — to scrutinize legal process more carefully.

Requests to Social Media Platforms

Significant Social Media Intermediaries (SSMIs) under IT Act rules must designate a Grievance Officer and Nodal Contact Person in India. The DPDP Act reinforces that legitimate government orders must be complied with. WhatsApp, Meta, Google, and X (Twitter) all have Indian nodal officers — investigators should route urgent requests through established LEA portals.

Data Localization

The DPDP Act does not impose blanket data localization requirements, but the government may restrict cross-border data flows for certain categories. This impacts cloud forensics — data stored on international servers may become harder to access without mutual legal assistance treaties (MLATs).

New Investigative Angle — DPDP Violations as Evidence

The DPDP Act creates a new investigative angle. Fraudsters who scrape and sell personal data — Aadhaar data, insurance data, hospital records — are now committing an offence under the DPDP Act in addition to existing IT Act provisions. Penalties are severe: up to ₹250 crore for data breaches caused by negligence.

Compliance Obligations for Investigation Teams

Investigation teams that process personal data — creating suspect profiles, maintaining victim databases, sharing intelligence — must be aware of the following:

  • Data must be used only for the specified investigation purpose
  • Data must not be retained beyond the period necessary for the investigation
  • Sharing investigation data with unauthorized parties may violate the Act
  • Security safeguards must be implemented for databases containing personal data

Penalties Relevant to Cybercriminals

ViolationMaximum Penalty

Conclusion

The DPDP Act does not significantly restrict cybercrime investigation — law enforcement exemptions are broad. However, it does create new offences that investigators should be aware of, and it increases the complexity of obtaining data from private companies. The most significant practical impact will be felt in international data requests and cloud forensics, where data localization rules and cross-border transfer restrictions will require updated MLAT procedures.
