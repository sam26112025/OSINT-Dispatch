Digital Evidence in India: How to Preserve It So It Holds Up in Court

An investigation can be brilliant and still collapse in the courtroom — not because the evidence was wrong, but because it was not preserved properly. Digital evidence is uniquely fragile: it can be altered in a click, it carries no obvious sign of tampering, and Indian courts rightly demand proof that what is shown to them is exactly what was seized. Getting this right is less about expensive tools and more about discipline. Here is the workflow that keeps electronic evidence defensible, framed for the Indian legal context under the new criminal codes.

The golden rule: never work on the original

The single most important principle in digital forensics is that you do not investigate the original. You create a verified copy (a forensic image) and work on that, leaving the seized device or data untouched and sealed. Every examination, every search, every export happens on the copy. If the original is ever needed, it is pristine. Acting directly on the original — opening files, “just checking” a phone — is the fastest way to taint a case.

Integrity by hashing

A cryptographic hash — SHA-256 is the practical standard — is a fixed-length fingerprint of a file or image. Change a single bit and the hash changes completely. The discipline is simple:

  • Compute and record the hash of the evidence at the moment of seizure/imaging.
  • Re-compute it after copying to confirm the copy is bit-for-bit identical.
  • Be able to reproduce that same hash at any later stage to prove nothing changed.

A matching hash is the most persuasive, objective answer to “how do we know this wasn't altered?” You can generate SHA-256 and other hashes entirely offline in the IGRS Toolkit without uploading anything.

Chain of custody: the unbroken story

Chain of custody is the documented, continuous record of the evidence's life: who seized it, when and where, who held it, who examined it, and every transfer in between. Each handover is logged and signed. A single unexplained gap — an hour where no one can say where the device was — is an opening for the defence to argue the evidence could have been substituted or modified. Maintain a custody log from the first minute, store evidence sealed and access-controlled, and record actions contemporaneously, not from memory afterwards.

The Section 63 BSA certificate (the old 65B)

This is where many otherwise-solid cases falter. Under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, Section 63 governs the admissibility of electronic records — the successor to the well-known Section 65B of the Indian Evidence Act. As a rule, an electronic record produced from a device must be accompanied by a certificate that identifies the record, describes how it was produced, and attests that the device was operating properly. Indian courts have repeatedly treated this certificate as essential for secondary electronic evidence. Prepare it correctly and contemporaneously; a missing or defective certificate is one of the most common reasons electronic evidence is excluded.

Document everything, in the right order

Good forensic reporting follows the evidence: Input (what was received and its hash), Analysis (what was done, with which tools and versions), Output (what was found, with artefacts), and Conclusion (what it supports, and the limits). Photograph the scene and device state, note timestamps and time zones, and keep your working copy separate from your exhibits. The aim is that an independent examiner could repeat your steps and reach the same result.

The mistakes that sink cases

  • Working on the original device or data instead of a verified image.
  • No hash at seizure — integrity becomes impossible to prove later.
  • A broken or undocumented chain of custody.
  • A missing, late, or defective Section 63 (formerly 65B) certificate.
  • Ignoring time-zone and clock-drift issues on devices, creating timeline contradictions.

Why this matters

In India's move to the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam of 2023, electronic evidence has only grown in importance — the codes expressly recognise digital records and digital processes. That makes preservation discipline a core investigative skill, not an afterthought for the forensic lab. The investigator who hashes early, never touches the original, keeps a clean custody log, and prepares the certificate correctly is the one whose evidence survives cross-examination.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Section 63 BSA certificate?
Under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, Section 63 governs admissibility of electronic records and requires a certificate (successor to Section 65B) attesting how the record was produced and that the source device worked properly. Without it, electronic evidence is often inadmissible.
Why is a hash value important?
A cryptographic hash such as SHA-256 is a digital fingerprint. Recording it at seizure and showing the same hash later proves the evidence was not altered — central to establishing integrity in court.
What is chain of custody?
The documented, unbroken record of who handled the evidence, when, where and why, from seizure to court. Any unexplained gap lets the defence argue tampering.
What is the most common reason digital evidence is rejected?
A missing or defective Section 63 (formerly 65B) certificate, working on the original instead of a verified copy, and a broken chain of custody are the most common reasons it is challenged or excluded.
